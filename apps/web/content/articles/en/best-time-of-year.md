The aurora does not switch off in summer. It is there all year round, but you need a dark sky to see it, and in Northern Norway the sky is only dark enough from about late August to mid-April. Within that window every month has its trade-offs: autumn and late winter combine decent darkness with slightly better activity statistics, while the polar night gives the most dark hours but often more cloud.

## Why summer is out

North of the Arctic Circle the sun does not set in summer. Tromsø has the midnight sun from about 18 May to 25 July, according to the Norwegian encyclopaedia Store norske leksikon, and further north it lasts longer still: at the North Cape (Nordkapp) from 12 May to 1 August. Even after the sun starts setting again, the night sky stays too bright for weeks. A blue twilight lingers around midnight.

What matters is how far below the horizon the sun is. When the centre of the sun is 6° below the horizon, civil twilight ends; at 12° nautical twilight ends, and at 18° the sky is fully dark (astronomical night). Faint aurora needs roughly nautical darkness, while a strong outburst can show up in a brighter sky.

We calculated the sun's position for Tromsø for the 2026–27 season with a standard astronomical ephemeris:

- The sun dips more than 6° below the horizon at midnight from about mid-August.
- Nautical darkness around midnight from about 1 September.
- Fully dark nights (below 18°) from about mid-September.
- In spring, fully dark nights disappear around 25 March, nautical darkness around 10 April, and the last usable hours towards the end of April.

That matches Visit Tromsø, which says the aurora can be seen from late August to early April. In Lofoten, Vesterålen and Finnmark the dates differ by only a few days.

## Polar night: the most hours of darkness

During the polar night (Norwegian: mørketid, literally "the dark time") the sun does not rise at all. In Tromsø it lasts from about 26 November to 17 January, in Vardø from 22 November to 21 January, and at the North Cape from 19 November to 24 January (Store norske leksikon; dates can shift by a day from year to year).

Polar night does not mean a black sky around the clock. Around midday there is a muted blue light, and aurora is rarely seen until the afternoon has turned fully dark. But from mid-afternoon until well into the morning the sky is dark enough. In mid-December Tromsø has about 16 hours of nautical darkness per day, compared with around 6 hours in mid-September.

The downside is the weather. Midwinter can be cold and stormy, especially on the coast, and roads can be demanding. Many polar-night visitors find they have plenty of darkness but too few clear evenings.

## The equinox effect: why March and September–October get praise

Already in the 19th century scientists noticed that disturbances of Earth's magnetic field are more common around the spring and autumn equinoxes than in midwinter or midsummer. The US space weather service NOAA SWPC notes a tendency for larger geomagnetic storms, and therefore better aurora, near the equinoxes.

The usual explanation is the Russell–McPherron effect, described by Christopher Russell and Robert McPherron in 1973. In short, how easily the solar wind couples to Earth's magnetic field depends on the angle between the solar wind's magnetic field and Earth's magnetic axis, and that geometry is most favourable around the equinoxes. Why the direction of that field matters so much is explained in [solar wind, Bz and speed](id:solar-wind).

Two important caveats:

- The effect is statistical. One strong solar storm in December beats an average March.
- Around the spring equinox darkness shrinks fast. Tromsø has about 8 hours of nautical darkness in mid-March and almost none by mid-April.

## Month by month

| Month | Darkness (Tromsø, mid-month) | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Late August | Short, bright nights | Mild, usually no snow, few visitors | Only strong aurora is visible, late in the evening |
| September | About 6 hours of nautical darkness | Equinox, autumn colours, open roads | Short nights, unsettled autumn weather |
| October | About 10–11 hours | Good darkness, often still mild | Autumn low-pressure systems with rain and wind |
| November | About 14 hours | Long nights | Often grey and wet, transition to winter |
| December | About 16 hours | Polar night, longest darkness | Often cloudy, cold, demanding roads |
| January | About 15 hours | Long nights, snowy landscapes | Cold, especially inland |
| February | About 12 hours | Daylight returns, a good balance of light and dark | Cold, especially inland |
| March | About 8 hours | Equinox, often more clear evenings inland, longer days | Shorter nights towards the end of the month |
| First half of April | Little | Bright days, few visitors | Only the first weeks, and only late at night |

The darkness figures are our own calculations for Tromsø; Finnmark and Lofoten are close to the same numbers.

## Clouds decide more than the calendar

The most important factor all season is not the month but whether the sky is clear where you are standing. A quick look at historical weather data (the ERA5 reanalysis via Open-Meteo, evening hours 2014–2025) confirms the pattern many locals know: November and December have the fewest clear evening hours across Northern Norway, while March comes out clearly best on the Finnmark plateau (Finnmarksvidda). The data are coarse, so use them as a pointer, not a verdict. Read more in [clouds and clear skies](id:clouds-weather).

The moon matters too. A full moon makes the aurora look paler to the eye, and in midwinter the full moon can stay up all night in Northern Norway. More on that in [best time of night](id:best-time-of-night).

## What about the solar cycle?

The solar cycle lasts about 11 years, and the current cycle most likely peaked around 2024–2025. That does not mean the aurora disappears in the coming years: the declining phase often brings repeated, moderate storms from coronal holes, and big storms can still happen. Read more in [the solar cycle](id:solar-cycle). In Northern Norway, activity is not the main constraint anyway, because the auroral oval often sits right over the region even on quiet nights (see [the Kp index](id:kp-index)).

## In short

- The season in Northern Norway runs from late August to mid-April.
- September–October and February–March are often the best compromise between darkness, weather and activity.
- The polar night gives the most dark hours, but often more cloud and tougher winter conditions.
- Book several nights if you can: your chances lie in the number of clear evenings, not in one particular date.

Finnmark is Norway's largest and most varied aurora region: the fjord at Alta, the open plateau around Karasjok and Kautokeino, the border town of Kirkenes, and the North Cape (Nordkapp) facing the Barents Sea. The whole county sits under the auroral oval on ordinary nights. What sets Finnmark apart from the coast further south and west is the climate: the interior is drier and much colder. And it was in Alta that systematic aurora research began.

## Well placed under the oval

By our calculation with the AACGM-v2 coordinate model, Alta lies at about 67.4° magnetic latitude, Karasjok and Kirkenes at about 67°. That is similar to Tromsø, and well inside the zone where aurora is common even when the Kp index is low. Read more in [the Kp index](id:kp-index).

Because Finnmark lies further east, magnetic midnight comes a little earlier than in Tromsø: in Alta at about 10:45–11:20 pm Norwegian winter time, in Kirkenes at about 10:25–10:55 pm. That is when the strongest outbursts most often happen; see [best time of night](id:best-time-of-night).

## Inland climate: dry, cold and different from the coast

The Norwegian encyclopaedia Store norske leksikon describes Alta's climate as continental: dry and warm in summer, cold in winter, with little precipitation. The interior on the Finnmark plateau (Finnmarksvidda) is colder still in winter: the lowest temperature ever measured in Norway, minus 51.4 °C, was recorded in Karasjok on 1 January 1886.

What does that mean for aurora hunting?

- **Less precipitation does not automatically mean clear skies**, but the interior is often under different weather systems from the coast. If it is cloudy in Hammerfest or Tromsø, it may be clear on the plateau, and vice versa.
- **Late winter is often good.** In a coarse historical weather dataset (the ERA5 reanalysis, evening hours 2014–2025), Karasjok and Kautokeino have clearly the most clear evening hours in March, while November and December are the cloudiest months. See [best time of year](id:best-season).
- **The cold is real.** Clear winter nights on the plateau can get extremely cold. Dress in layers, protect fingers and face, and always carry warm clothes and enough fuel in the car. See [safety and clothing](id:safety-clothing).

## Alta: where aurora research began

Alta Museum tells a story that makes the town special for anyone interested in the aurora:

- A French expedition, the Recherche expedition, studied the aurora at the Alta fjord in 1838–39. Its work later drew other scientists to Alta.
- The oldest surviving photograph of the aurora was taken by the German scientist Martin Brendel at Bossekop in Alta on 5 January 1892.
- Kristian Birkeland persuaded the Norwegian parliament to build an observatory on Mount Halde, about 900 metres above sea level. It opened in 1899 and is described as the world's first aurora observatory. The polar explorer Roald Amundsen was among those who stayed there, in the winter of 1902–03.
- Operations moved to Tromsø in 1926. German occupying forces burned the observatory in 1944, and the buildings were restored from the 1980s onwards.

If you want to see the place yourself, a T-marked path and cart road runs from Kåfjord all the way to the top. Visit Alta gives the hike as about 9 km and six hours, in the summer and autumn season, and at the top you find the historic buildings and a bust of Birkeland. According to Visit Alta, the name Halde means sacred mountain in Sami. It is not a hike for a dark winter aurora night, but a fine day trip for anyone who wants to understand where the research started.

Today Alta uses the brand name Nordlysbyen, the City of Northern Lights, in tourism. The Northern Lights Cathedral (Nordlyskatedralen), the circular church in the town centre, was consecrated in 2013 and designed by LINK arkitektur together with Schmidt Hammer Lassen.

Alta town lies on flat ground at the mouth of the Alta valley, and you quickly leave the town lights behind along the fjord or up the valleys. Low light and an open view to the north matter most.

## Karasjok and Kautokeino: the plateau and the Sami heartland

Karasjok (Sami: Kárášjohka) and Kautokeino (Sami: Guovdageaidnu) lie on the Finnmark plateau, in the Sami heartland where reindeer herding is an important livelihood. The Sami Parliament (Sametinget) is based in Karasjok. Kautokeino is Norway's largest municipality by area, at around 9,700 square kilometres, and at Easter it hosts reindeer racing and a cultural festival.

The plateau is flat and open, with little light pollution outside the villages, and gives aurora from horizon to horizon. Two things to remember:

- Reindeer on and beside the road are common. Slow down, especially in the dark.
- You are a guest in a living Sami community. Respect reindeer herding, private land and cultural heritage sites. For Sami tradition and the aurora, see [myths and Sami traditions](id:myths-sami).

Distances are long: from Alta it is about 130 km by road to Kautokeino and about 200 km to Karasjok, and winter conditions make the drive slower.

## Kirkenes and eastern Finnmark

Kirkenes lies in the far east, near the Russian border, and is the end point of both the E6 highway and the Hurtigruten coastal route. Kirkenes Airport, Høybuktmoen, has scheduled flights, and the town is the gateway to the Pasvik valley. Magnetic midnight comes earliest here of the places in this article, and outside town it is a short way to dark skies. From Karasjok it is about 270 km by road.

## North Cape: mind the winter convoy

The North Cape is a classic, but winter takes planning. From winter 2025–26 the Norwegian Public Roads Administration (Statens vegvesen) introduced convoy driving on the E69 between the Skarsvåg junction and the North Cape plateau. The convoy starts when the weather requires it and lasts until spring. In the 2025–26 timetable the last convoy out left at 7:30 pm and the last one back at 9:30 pm, before magnetic midnight. Check the current times for winter 2026–27, and consider watching the aurora around Honningsvåg instead, where you are free to wait. From Alta it is about 205 km to Honningsvåg.

## In short

- All of Finnmark sits under the auroral oval on ordinary nights; magnetic midnight comes a little earlier than in Tromsø.
- The inland climate is dry and cold, and the weather is often different from the coast, which gives you options.
- Alta has the world's oldest aurora research history, with the Halde observatory from 1899.
- The plateau offers open skies, but also severe cold, reindeer on the road and long distances.

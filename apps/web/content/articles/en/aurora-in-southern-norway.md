Yes, you can see the aurora from Oslo, Bergen, Trondheim and the rest of southern Norway, but it takes more than an ordinary aurora evening in Tromsø. You need raised geomagnetic activity, often Kp 4 to 5 or more, plus darkness and clear skies. And you need to look north: most of the time the aurora sits low over the northern horizon, not straight overhead.

## Why the aurora is rarer in the south

The aurora follows a ring around the magnetic north pole, the [auroral oval](id:what-is-aurora). What decides how close you are to the oval is not your ordinary latitude but your magnetic latitude, and southern Norway lies several degrees of magnetic latitude south of where the oval usually sits.

NOAA gives a rough relationship: at Kp 0 the southern edge of the oval lies at about 66 degrees magnetic latitude, and it moves about two degrees south for every step on the [Kp index](id:kp-index). NOAA itself stresses that these are averages, not exact limits. The table below combines that rule with magnetic latitudes we calculated using the AACGM coordinate model. It shows roughly when the aurora can reach overhead. Low in the north, you can often see it at a lower Kp.

| Place | Magnetic latitude (approx.) | Kp for aurora high in the sky (rough estimate) |
|---|---|---|
| Trondheim | 61° | 2–3 |
| Lillehammer | 58.5° | 4 |
| Bergen | 58° | 4 |
| Oslo | 57° | 4–5 |
| Stavanger | 56° | 5 |
| Kristiansand | 55° | 5–6 |

In 2022 meteorologist Eldbjørg Moxnes of the Norwegian Meteorological Institute (MET Norway) gave the broadcaster NRK a simpler rule of thumb for seeing the aurora at all: at least Kp 4 in Oslo and at least Kp 5 in Kristiansand, against Kp 0 in Tromsø. That fits the table well once you allow for the aurora being visible low in the north before it reaches overhead.

Treat these numbers as a guide. Kp is a three-hour measure for the whole planet, and a strong burst can come and go within a single Kp period. More in [how to read an aurora forecast](id:read-forecast).

## Nights when it happened

The Sun has been very active in recent years. When NRK reported on the November 2025 storm, it said activity had not been that high for 22 years. Some examples of aurora nights in the south:

- **Night of 10–11 May 2024**: The first G5 storm, the top level on NOAA's scale, in more than 20 years. NRK reported colourful aurora over southern Norway and much of Europe, with lots of pink and purple as well as green.
- **10 October 2024**: Another strong storm. ESA compared it with the May storm using its Swarm satellites, and both produced strong magnetic disturbances much further south than usual.
- **1 January 2025**: State meteorologist Magnus Haukeland in Bergen told NRK that the aurora rarely reaches as far south as Bergen, but that it was visible there and further south that evening.
- **Night of 11–12 November 2025**: NRK reported strong aurora across the whole country after several eruptions on the Sun, and people saw intense red aurora.

To understand what happens on the Sun before nights like these, read about [geomagnetic storms](id:geomagnetic-storms) and [the solar cycle](id:solar-cycle).

## What the aurora looks like from the south

From southern Norway you usually do not see the aurora overhead. You see it from the side, low in the north. NOAA estimates that from a high point with a clear view north you can see aurora up to 1,000 km further north.

That gives it a few typical traits:

- **A low green band or a glow in the north.** In moderate activity it often looks like a pale grey-green arc or a glow above the horizon, easy to mistake for city light or thin cloud.
- **Red and pink.** Red aurora forms higher in the atmosphere than green. The Fram Centre gives typical heights of around 120 km for green aurora and around 250 km for red. The higher the light, the further away it can be seen above the horizon, which is why people in the south often catch the red upper parts of the display. See [aurora colours](id:aurora-colours).
- **Your camera sees more than you do.** The eye is poor at colour in the dark, while a camera collects light for several seconds. Many people first spot the aurora on their screen, so take a test shot towards the north if you are unsure. Tips in [photographing the aurora with a phone](id:phone-photo).

During the truly big storms, like May 2024, the aurora can also climb high into the sky in the south. NOAA says that at Kp 8 to 9 the aurora may be seen overhead in the northern states of the USA, which by our calculation sit at roughly the same magnetic latitude as southern Norway. Nights like that are rare.

## Where to go

What matters most is an open view to the north with as little light as possible in that direction. Some practical tips:

- **Get out of town.** From Oslo that usually means heading north into the forests and hills, so the city glow is behind you rather than on the northern horizon. NRK has published photos of aurora over Holmenkollen, the ski jump hill above Oslo, but darker is always better.
- **Find height or open water.** A hilltop, a beach or a lake with a view north gives you sky all the way down to the horizon.
- **The mountains are best.** High mountain areas such as the Hardangervidda plateau or the mountains of Valdres and Jotunheimen have little light pollution and often clearer air. Winter roads can be demanding, so check road conditions first and read our [safety and clothing](id:safety-clothing) advice.
- **Be considerate.** Do not park where you block roads or driveways, and switch off your headlights when others are taking photos.

## How to catch the night

The biggest aurora nights in the south rarely arrive without warning. The Fram Centre notes that particles from the Sun can take two to three days to reach Earth, so after a large eruption there is often a forecast a couple of days ahead. But the timing is uncertain, and what matters often happens at short notice.

1. **Watch the alerts** when a storm is forecast, and [check tonight's verdict for your location](app:) through the evening.
2. **Check the clouds.** Moxnes pointed out in 2022 that cloud forecasts several days ahead are quite uncertain. Look at the cloud forecast on the evening itself, and read [clouds and clear skies](id:clouds-weather).
3. **Follow real-time data.** When the solar wind is strong and the magnetic field points south, the aurora can flare up quickly. See [solar wind, Bz and speed](id:solar-wind).
4. **Be patient.** Astrophysicist Knut Jørgen Røed Ødegaard told NRK in 2025 that strong aurora tends to come in waves lasting half an hour to an hour. Give it at least an hour before you give up.

## In short

- Aurora in southern Norway needs raised activity: roughly Kp 4 for Oslo and Kp 5 for Kristiansand by MET Norway's rule of thumb, more to get it high in the sky.
- Look north, from a dark spot with an open horizon.
- Red and pink aurora is common from the south, and the camera often sees more than the eye.
- Big storms are often forecast a couple of days ahead, but the clouds and the last few hours decide the night.

The aurora (Norwegian: *nordlys*, "northern light") is light from the upper atmosphere. It appears when electrically charged particles, mostly electrons, are guided by Earth's magnetic field towards the polar regions and collide with oxygen and nitrogen roughly 80 to 500 kilometres above the ground. The gases absorb energy and release it again as light, much like the glow of a fluorescent tube. Northern Norway sits almost directly under the belt where this happens most often, which is why your chances here are so good.

## From the Sun to the sky above you

It all starts with the Sun. It constantly sends out a stream of electrons and protons called the [solar wind](id:solar-wind). At Earth's distance from the Sun, the solar wind typically travels at a few hundred kilometres per second, and it drags part of the Sun's magnetic field out into space with it.

Earth is protected by its own magnetic field. The region where Earth's field dominates is called the magnetosphere. The solar wind squeezes it on the day side, to about ten Earth radii from the planet's centre, and stretches it into a long tail on the night side, far beyond the Moon's orbit. The boundary is not sealed. Energy and particles leak in, especially when the magnetic field carried by the solar wind points southwards, opposite to Earth's own field. That is when the two fields couple most efficiently.

The energy is stored in the magnetotail. When it is released, electrons are accelerated along magnetic field lines down into the atmosphere, at around a tenth of the speed of light according to NOAA. It is these electrons, not particles arriving straight from the Sun, that light up most of the aurora we see at night.

## How the air starts to glow

When a fast electron hits an oxygen atom or a nitrogen molecule, it lifts the atom into a higher energy state. After a short while the atom drops back and emits a photon, a particle of light with a specific wavelength. The colour depends on which gas is hit and at what height.

- The most common colour is green, from atomic oxygen at 557.7 nanometres.
- Red also comes from oxygen (630.0 nm), but higher up, where the air is thinnest.
- A purple or pink lower fringe comes from nitrogen, when the electrons are energetic enough to dive deep.

Our article on [the colours of the aurora](id:aurora-colours) goes into this in detail and explains why your camera often sees more colour than your eyes.

## How high is the aurora?

The aurora is far above the weather and the clouds. The Norwegian scientist Carl Størmer measured its height by photographing the same auroral forms at the same moment from two sites a known distance apart. More than 14,000 measurements showed that the brightest night-time aurora usually lies between 100 and 120 kilometres up. Its lower edge can occasionally reach down to about 85 km, while rays may stretch to 200–300 km. A more recent study using all-sky cameras in northern Finland and Sweden found that both the green and the blue emission typically peak at around 114 km.

This means that an aurora you see low on the northern horizon from Trondheim or Oslo can be almost overhead for people several hundred kilometres further north.

## The auroral oval: why Northern Norway is so well placed

The magnetic field funnels the particles into a ring-shaped belt around each magnetic pole, called the auroral oval. The oval stays fixed relative to the Sun while Earth turns beneath it. According to the Norwegian encyclopedia Store norske leksikon, on the night side the oval lies about 23 degrees from the magnetic pole, and closer to the pole on the day side. In our part of the world it follows the coast of Troms and Finnmark at night, while in daytime it lies over Svalbard.

The zone where the aurora appears most often is called the auroral zone. There, a clearly visible aurora occurs on more than half of all nights, even when the Sun is quiet. NOAA says the same: under the oval, roughly between latitudes 60 and 75 degrees, the aurora can be seen on more than half the nights of the year, as long as it is dark and clear.

When activity rises, the oval expands southwards and widens. That is why the aurora can sometimes be seen from Oslo, Bergen and further south. The strength of the disturbance is measured with, among other things, the [Kp index](id:kp-index). During [geomagnetic storms](id:geomagnetic-storms) the oval can reach deep into Europe.

## Shapes and phases: arcs, curtains and the corona

The aurora has a few basic forms that keep coming back:

- **Arcs and bands** stretch across the sky in a magnetic east–west direction. Arcs are often calm and even; bands can fold.
- **Rays and curtains** follow the magnetic field lines and stand almost vertical at Norwegian latitudes. The curtain shape inspired the logo of the 1994 Winter Olympics in Lillehammer.
- **The corona** is when rays seem to burst from a single point straight above you. It is perspective: you are looking up along the field lines.
- **Diffuse patches** are most common late at night and towards morning, and they often pulse on and off.

An active evening typically follows a pattern called an auroral substorm. First you see one or more faint, yellow-green arcs low in the north, which can last for hours. Then the arcs brighten, develop rays and drift south. Suddenly comes the breakup: the light spreads across much of the sky in bands and curtains that twist quickly, often with a red or purple lower edge. This phase usually lasts about ten minutes. Afterwards things calm down into a diffuse, pulsing veil. The sequence can repeat several times in one night, and the most dynamic aurora comes around magnetic midnight. ## A Norwegian science story

The aurora has fascinated people for thousands of years, and the Norwegian word has deep roots: the King's Mirror (*Kongespeilet*), written in Norway around 1230, is according to an article published by the US National Park Service the first known text to use the term "northern lights". In 1741 the Swedish scientist Anders Celsius found a link between the aurora and disturbances in Earth's magnetic field.

The big breakthrough was Norwegian. Kristian Birkeland proposed that the aurora is caused by charged particles from the Sun captured by Earth's magnetic field. Around 1900 he built a magnetised model globe, a *terrella*, inside a vacuum chamber and produced glowing rings around its poles. He also set up his own observatories in the Arctic. Carl Størmer worked out the particle paths mathematically and mapped the aurora's height. Birkeland and the auroral oval feature on the Norwegian 200-krone banknote issued in 1994. Modern rocket and satellite measurements have largely confirmed the main points of Birkeland's theory.

## In short

- The aurora is light from oxygen and nitrogen struck by electrons, typically 100–120 km above the ground.
- Its energy comes from the solar wind, and it couples in best when the solar wind's magnetic field points south.
- Northern Norway lies under the auroral oval at night, where the aurora is overhead on more than half of all nights. What you also need is darkness and a clear sky; see [clouds and clear skies](id:clouds-weather).
